\chapter{Discussion}
\label{chap:discussion}

This chapter interprets the empirical findings of Chapter~\ref{chap:results} in broader context. Section~\ref{sec:disc_exchangeability} discusses the exchangeability assumption underlying conformal coverage and its relevance to the temporally ordered SPX options data. Section~\ref{sec:disc_practical} considers the practical interpretation of the resulting band surfaces. Section~\ref{sec:disc_limitations} discusses the limitations of the current study, and Section~\ref{sec:disc_future} outlines focused directions for future research. Section~\ref{sec:disc_summary} summarises the main points.

\section{The Exchangeability Assumption in Practice}
\label{sec:disc_exchangeability}

The standard finite-sample coverage guarantee of conformal prediction relies on exchangeability of the calibration and test nonconformity scores, meaning that their joint distribution is invariant to permutations of their indices \cite{shafer2008tutorial,angelopoulos2023gentle}. Independent and identically distributed observations provide a common sufficient condition, but exchangeability is the more general requirement. In the present application, option surfaces are ordered in time and the quotes within a surface are related through the same market state. Exact exchangeability is therefore not assumed. The rolling procedure is used as a practical adaptation mechanism, and its coverage is evaluated empirically in Chapter~\ref{chap:results}.

\subsection{Serial Dependence and the Rolling Window}
\label{subsec:disc_serial}

The rolling window restricts calibration to the $K=5$ most recently processed surfaces. This keeps the calibration information temporally close to the surface being evaluated and prevents distant observations from remaining in the window indefinitely. The window counts available surfaces rather than elapsed calendar days, which is appropriate for the irregular sequence of successfully obtained snapshots used in this thesis. However, restricting the window does not by itself establish exchangeability or restore the formal finite-sample guarantee under temporal dependence. It instead provides a simple way to make the conformal quantile responsive to recently observed errors, consistent with the motivation for giving recent observations greater relevance under distribution drift \cite{barber2023conformal}.

The Score B global quantile remains within a relatively narrow range during the initial calibration period. It moves from 0.01623 after the first surface to 0.01769 after the final calibration surface, with intermediate values between 0.01568 and 0.01781. This shows that the rolling estimate changes as surfaces enter and leave the window without being dominated by a single calibration date. The calibration behaviour alone cannot verify exchangeability, but it shows that the selected window produces a usable and gradually adapting threshold for the subsequent walk-forward evaluation.

\subsection{Temporal Variation in Coverage}
\label{subsec:disc_regime}

Across the 41 evaluation surfaces, post-projection global coverage is below the 90\% target on 18 surfaces, while Mondrian coverage is below 90\% on 10 surfaces. The shortfalls are not confined to one part of the evaluation period. For example, surface 19 has global coverage of 88.94\%, which increases to 91.52\% with Mondrian assignment, whereas surface 54 remains below target at 86.12\% globally and 87.56\% with Mondrian assignment. These results show that the relevance of the preceding rolling window varies over time.

At each walk-forward step, the current surface is evaluated before its scores are added to the window. The procedure can therefore adapt after a new error pattern has been observed, but it cannot anticipate a change that is not represented in the preceding surfaces. The available data do not include a separate analysis of market regimes or other covariates that would identify the cause of each coverage shortfall. The results are therefore interpreted as empirical temporal variation rather than attributed to a specific market event. Barber et al.\ \cite{barber2023conformal} quantify how departures from exchangeability can reduce coverage through bounds based on differences between original and index-swapped data or residual distributions. This supports the cautious interpretation adopted here: the rolling method is useful empirically, but the standard exchangeable-data guarantee is not claimed for the time-dependent evaluation.

\subsection{Contribution of Mondrian Binning}
\label{subsec:disc_mondrian_remedy}

Mondrian binning raises mean post-projection coverage from 90.23\% for the global construction to 91.34\%. On 11 surfaces where global coverage is below 90\%, the Mondrian construction raises coverage to at least the target. It does not improve every surface, and ten surfaces remain below 90\%. The effect is therefore beneficial on average rather than uniform across time.

The calibration results help explain this improvement. At the final calibration surface, the bin-specific Score B quantiles range from 0.011950 to 0.040613, showing substantial spatial variation around the global quantile of 0.017689. Assigning these thresholds by $(\rho,z)$ region allows the band width to respond to the observed spatial distribution of errors. Mondrian conformal prediction is motivated by calibration within predefined categories under exchangeability \cite{vovk2005algorithmic,shafer2008tutorial}. In this thesis, the rolling window and shrinkage toward the global quantile make the construction an empirical spatial adaptation rather than a claim of exact conditional coverage.

\section{Practical Implications}
\label{sec:disc_practical}

The framework produces complete lower and upper surfaces around the smooth GNO estimate over the modelled domain. The conformal calibration is performed using errors at observed quote locations, after which interpolation and projection provide the surface representation. Empirical coverage is evaluated at the observed quotes, where market implied volatility is available for comparison. This combines a surface-level output with a directly measurable quote-level evaluation.

The width of the band provides an interpretable indication of uncertainty around the GNO surface. For Score B, the mean IV width is 0.0273 and the corresponding mean half-width is approximately 0.0137, compared with a GNO mean absolute error of 0.006789. The bands may therefore support model monitoring by showing the range around the point surface that is consistent with recent prediction errors. Wider regions indicate where the calibrated uncertainty is larger, while narrower regions indicate greater agreement with the recent calibration window. The bands should be interpreted as model uncertainty estimates rather than as direct trading or hedging recommendations.

The comparison of the two scores supports the use of Score B for the present application. Score A achieves higher mean coverage of 94.76\%, but its mean IV width of 0.1491 is approximately 5.5 times the Score B width and its projected surfaces retain residual calendar and butterfly violations. Score B achieves mean Mondrian coverage of 91.34\%, reaches zero residual counts for the implemented calendar and butterfly conditions on all evaluation surfaces, and has zero lower--upper crossings. It therefore provides the more balanced combination of coverage, band width, and financial shape adjustment in this dataset.

The projection operates on the complete lower and upper surfaces rather than adjusting isolated quote intervals. For Score B, the resulting surfaces satisfy the two implemented discrete conditions on the evaluation grids while preserving their lower--upper ordering at the observed quotes. This makes the projected bands easier to use as coherent surface objects in subsequent analysis.

\section{Limitations}
\label{sec:disc_limitations}

The empirical study uses one underlying asset, one pre-trained GNO checkpoint, and 56 successfully collected SPX option surface snapshots. The 41-surface evaluation covers collection dates from 29 January to 15 August 2026, but the sequence is irregular because provider-side API errors and timeouts prevented successful retrieval on some scheduled dates. The retrieval script records the collection date in the saved schema, rather than a separate exchange-provided quote timestamp. The dates therefore provide a chronological index for the successfully collected chains and should not be interpreted as a complete sample of trading days. The results provide evidence for this dataset and period and should not be assumed to transfer unchanged to other underlyings, models, or market periods.

The rolling window size $K=5$, the $4\times4$ Mondrian partition, and shrinkage parameter $\lambda=0.3$ are held fixed throughout the reported experiments. Alternative configurations were considered during preliminary trials, and the selected values provided the best overall balance of coverage, band width, and stability for the available data. The thesis therefore uses this configuration for the comparison of global and Mondrian calibration and of Score A and Score B. A systematic sensitivity analysis over all possible window lengths, bin counts, and shrinkage values remains outside the scope of the present study.

Coverage is measured over the observed quotes on each evaluation surface and then summarised across surfaces. Quotes within the same surface are cross-sectionally related, and regions containing more observed quotes contribute more observations to that surface's coverage value. Together with temporal dependence between surfaces, this reinforces the use of empirical coverage as the main assessment rather than treating the standard exchangeability result as exact for this dataset.

The calendar and butterfly adjustments are implemented as discrete conditions on a finite $(\rho,z)$ grid. The calendar step operates across maturities at fixed $z$, matching the transformed coordinate representation used by the implementation. A fixed-$k$ calendar calculation is retained as a supplementary diagnostic rather than an enforced condition. Similarly, the butterfly step applies the implemented chord condition across $z$ at each maturity. These choices provide a stable and transparent surface adjustment, while alternative grid formulations could be considered in a broader implementation.

The lower and upper surfaces are projected independently. No lower--upper crossings are observed in the reported experiments, but the independent procedure does not impose their joint ordering as part of a single optimisation problem. The zero crossing count is therefore an empirical result of the current evaluation.

\section{Future Research}
\label{sec:disc_future}

A first extension would evaluate the framework on a longer sequence of SPX surfaces and on additional index or single-name option datasets. This would show whether the relative performance of Score A and Score B, the selected band widths, and the behaviour of the rolling calibration remain similar across other assets and periods.

A second extension would examine sensitivity to the main calibration choices, including the rolling window length, the number of Mondrian bins, and the shrinkage parameter. Weighted or adaptive conformal methods could also be considered if stronger adaptation to temporal distribution drift is required \cite{barber2023conformal}. These extensions would complement the current fixed configuration rather than alter the conclusions obtained from the reported experiment.

A further implementation extension could formulate the calendar adjustment directly on a fixed-$k$ grid and compare it with the current fixed-$z$ construction. The lower and upper surfaces could also be projected jointly so that their ordering is imposed directly. Finally, comparisons with additional volatility-surface or uncertainty baselines would provide a broader assessment of computational and statistical trade-offs.

\section{Summary}
\label{sec:disc_summary}
\enlargethispage{2\baselineskip}

The empirical results show that rolling calibration and Mondrian spatial binning provide a practical uncertainty layer around the pre-trained GNO, although the temporal structure of the data means that the standard exchangeability guarantee is not treated as exact. Score B provides the preferred balance in the reported experiment: its mean coverage is above the nominal target, its bands are substantially narrower than those of Score A, and the implemented calendar and butterfly conditions have zero residual counts after projection on all evaluation surfaces. The limitations of the data scope, fixed calibration choices, and discrete projection define clear extensions without changing the main conclusion of the thesis.
